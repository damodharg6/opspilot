Four incidents, in plain English

5 min read · real recordings from 24 October 2026

We built a small online shop that sells car parts, then broke it on purpose in four different ways. Each time, an alarm went off and OpsPilot investigated on its own. Here is what happened, told as stories, with the real numbers.

How each test ran Two minutes of normal shoppers (about six visits a second), then a fault switched on for another one to one and a half minutes while shopping continued, then the alarm. OpsPilot was told only "the shop looks unhealthy". It had to find the cause itself.

The four incidents

  1. A release breaks checkout
  2. Everything is slow, nothing is broken
  3. Not an outage at all
  4. Memory quietly climbing
  5. What the four have in common
Incident 1 · buggy deploy

A release breaks checkout

A new version of the shop, "v1.5.0", went out with a change to how order prices are calculated. It added promotions, but only knew the discounts for two of the four products. Orders for the other two crashed.

What customers saw
Before: 724 visits, every one worked. After: 79 of 352 visits failed with an error page, all of them when placing an order.
Clues checked
7, in this order: counted the errors, read the change log, checked the error rate and speed graphs, opened the new release to read exactly what changed, then searched the diary for the failing orders.
Conclusion
Bad deploy. The new release's pricing code crashes for products without a promotion. Suggested fix: undo the release, or make the code use "no discount" when a product has none.
Time and confidence
About 32 seconds. Medium confidence: one quote in its first draft didn't match the evidence exactly, so it was removed and confidence lowered.

Why it matters: this is the most common kind of outage. The useful part is the link between "errors started" and "this exact change went out", which a person would normally piece together by hand.

Incident 2 · slow database

Everything is slow, nothing is broken

The database, the shop's filing cabinet, started taking over a second to answer each question. Nothing crashed. Pages just loaded very slowly.

What customers saw
Zero errors. But a typical page went from about 10 milliseconds to 1.2 seconds, more than 100 times slower.
Clues checked
4: counted errors (none, so that's not it), looked at the speed graphs, then read the diary for slow requests.
Conclusion
Database slowness. Requests still succeed but take over 1.2 seconds. Suggested next step: look for slow queries, locking or an overloaded database.
Time and confidence
About 34 seconds. Medium confidence, again after one unproven quote was removed.

Why it matters: a tool that only counts errors would say "all fine". Customers would disagree. OpsPilot noticed that the error count was zero and moved on to speed.

Incident 3 · stock exhaustion

Not an outage at all

One product, the front brake pads, sold out. Customers trying to buy them got a polite "insufficient stock" message. The systems were completely healthy. This is a business situation, not a technical failure.

What customers saw
32 "out of stock" messages out of 368 visits. Zero real errors, normal speed.
Clues checked
5: the error and speed graphs, then three searches of the diary.
Conclusion
Business issue, not an outage. The service is healthy; no technical action needed.
Time and confidence
About 63 seconds. High confidence, and every quote checked out.

Why it matters: a bad assistant would invent a technical cause and wake up the database team for nothing. Saying "this isn't an outage" is just as valuable as finding one.

Incident 4 · memory leak

Memory quietly climbing

Every visit made the shop hold on to a little extra memory and never let it go. Like a desk that collects paper and is never cleared, it works fine until suddenly there is no space left.

What customers saw
Nothing yet: all 544 visits worked at normal speed.
Clues checked
6: errors (none), three graphs including memory use, and two diary searches.
Conclusion
Memory problem. Memory use is growing abnormally. Suggested fix: stop the leak or restart the service.
Time and confidence
About 46 seconds. Medium confidence after one quote was removed.
Honest footnote Our demo shop writes a line in its diary when we switch a fault on, and OpsPilot quoted that line as evidence. A real system wouldn't announce its own leak, so in real life the memory graph would have to carry the argument alone. Hiding that line from the assistant is on our improvement list.

Why it matters: catching this before anything fails turns a middle-of-the-night outage into a daytime task.

What the four have in common

  • It started from the same vague alarm every time and still reached four different, correct answers.
  • It changed its approach to fit the evidence: commits for the release, speed for the database, memory for the leak.
  • It showed its work. Every report lists every clue it checked, and the receipts check removed unproven quotes in three of the four cases.
  • It knew when nothing was broken.
