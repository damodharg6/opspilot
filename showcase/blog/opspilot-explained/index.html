OpsPilot, explained without the jargon

6 min read · for anyone, no technical background needed

Every app you use, from food delivery to your bank, is looked after by people who get woken up when it breaks. OpsPilot is an AI assistant that takes the first look for them. Here is why that matters, how it works, where it helps, and who it helps.

Why: the 3 a.m. problem

Online services break. A new version goes out with a hidden mistake, a database gets slow, a server runs out of memory. When that happens, an automatic alarm goes off and a person's phone rings. That person is the on-call engineer, and it is often 3 a.m.

Before they can fix anything, they have to work out what is wrong. That means opening several tools, reading thousands of lines of records, comparing graphs and checking what changed recently, all while half asleep and while customers can't pay, order or log in. This first "what is going on?" phase often takes longer than the fix itself.

Think of it like this A car's warning light comes on. The light tells you something is wrong, not what. A mechanic still has to plug in a scanner, read the codes and check recent repairs before touching a single bolt. OpsPilot is that first diagnostic scan, done for you before you even reach the garage.

Every minute of an outage costs money: lost sales, refunds, support calls and lost trust. It also costs people: being woken up repeatedly is one of the main reasons engineers burn out and leave.

What OpsPilot is

OpsPilot is an AI on-call assistant. When an alarm fires, it starts investigating straight away, by itself. Within about a minute it produces a short report: what is most likely wrong, the evidence for it, how confident it is, and a suggested next step.

The person on call wakes up to a written explanation instead of a blank screen. They still make the decision. OpsPilot just does the first round of detective work.

The key idea OpsPilot can look at everything it needs but cannot change anything. It is a detective, not a mechanic. A human always decides what to do.

How it works, in five steps

  1. The alarm rings. A monitoring system notices something is off, for example "too many errors in the last few minutes", and tells OpsPilot.
  2. A case file is opened. OpsPilot writes the alarm down as an "incident" and replies instantly, "got it". If the same alarm rings ten times, it still opens just one case.
  3. The detective gathers clues. An AI model reads three kinds of evidence, like a doctor reading a patient's chart:
    • The diary (logs): a line written for every customer request, saying what happened and whether it worked.
    • The vital signs (metrics): numbers over time, like error rate, response speed and memory use.
    • The change log (deploys): which new versions went out recently, and exactly what they changed.
  4. It writes a report with receipts. Every claim in the report must be backed by a quote copied from a real clue. Before the report is saved, OpsPilot checks each quote against the actual evidence. Anything it can't find is thrown out, and the confidence goes down.
  5. A human decides. The on-call engineer reads the report and the full list of clues it checked, then acts: undo the release, fix the database, or simply relax because nothing is broken.

There are also strict limits on how long the assistant may investigate, how many clues it may request and how much AI text it may use. It can't go round in circles or run up a large bill.

Where it runs

  • On a laptop: the whole system, including a small demo shop that can be broken on purpose, starts with one command. That is how the demos on this site were recorded.
  • In the cloud (AWS): the project includes ready-made configuration to run it on Amazon Web Services, connected to Amazon's monitoring alarms.
  • With any AI provider: it works with several AI services (Google Gemini, Groq, OpenAI, or a model on your own computer). Switching is a settings change.

Where it helps

Anywhere an online service has to stay up and people are on call for it:

  • Online shops: checkout breaks during a sale; every minute is lost revenue.
  • Banking and payments: failed transfers need a fast, careful explanation, with evidence for auditors.
  • Software companies (SaaS): thousands of business customers depend on the app being up.
  • Games and streaming: huge traffic spikes at launch time.
  • Small teams and start-ups: no 24/7 operations team, so one developer is on call for everything.
  • Hospitals, logistics, public services: internal systems where downtime stops real work.

Why use it

  • Faster first look. The slowest part of an outage is often working out what is wrong. OpsPilot does that part before a human has even opened their laptop.
  • Calmer nights. Waking up to a clear explanation is very different from waking up to a red alert and nothing else.
  • Consistent. It checks the same clues in a sensible order every time, even at 3 a.m. and even on the hundredth alarm.
  • Honest about doubt. It states its confidence and shows its evidence, so people know how far to trust it.
  • Safe to try. Because it can only read, the worst a wrong report can do is waste a few minutes of attention.

Who it helps

  • On-call engineers: less searching, more fixing, and more sleep.
  • Managers: shorter outages, fewer escalations, and a written record of every incident.
  • Customers: things come back faster.
  • The business: less lost revenue, and less burnout in the team that keeps everything running.

What it is not

  • Not an autopilot. It never restarts, deletes, rolls back or changes anything. It recommends; people act.
  • Not a replacement for engineers. It is the junior colleague who has already pulled up the right charts when you arrive.
  • Not magic. If the clues aren't there (no traffic, no logs), it says "unknown" rather than inventing a cause.
